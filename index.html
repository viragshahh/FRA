METHODOLOGY RATIONALE

Why this universe and these dates?

Why 45 stocks from the NIFTY 100?

The 45 stocks were selected to capture diverse sectoral and risk profiles while ensuring sufficient historical data for consistent comparison across market regimes. This provides a manageable yet diversified universe for risk analysis, covariance estimation, and Markowitz portfolio optimization.

Why price history from 2018?

It provides a common history before the 2020 crisis and supports comparison across market periods.

Why adjusted closing prices?

They account for dividends and stock splits, making return calculations more consistent.

Why use the Nifty 50 benchmark?

It provides a market reference for beta and market movements; the 45 stocks are not meant to replicate the index.

03 • PORTFOLIO CONSTRUCTION

Risk scoring and stock selection

The study compares two ten stock portfolios formed from a composite score that combines market sensitivity, volatility and drawdown.

METHODOLOGY

How we build the composite risk score

We combine three complementary measures to rank the 45 selected stocks by relative risk.

Beta (30%)

Market sensitivity: how strongly a stock tends to move with the Nifty 50.

Annualized volatility (40%)

Overall return variability. It receives the highest weight because it captures broad fluctuations across market conditions.

Maximum drawdown (30%)

The largest peak-to-trough fall, adding a direct measure of downside experience.

Correlation check and ES exclusion

We compared the candidate metrics across all 45 stocks before finalizing the score. ES 99% correlated very strongly with annualized volatility (r = 0.9557). Including both could double-count similar risk information, so ES is excluded from this composite score—but remains useful for tail-loss analysis elsewhere in the project.

How the three metrics work together

Each metric is converted to a relative score from 0–100, then combined using the weights above:

Composite score = 0.30 × Beta + 0.40 × Volatility + 0.30 × Maximum Drawdown

Beta captures market-linked risk, volatility captures general return variability, and drawdown captures the worst peak-to-trough loss. Together they balance three different risk dimensions. The weights are transparent, judgment-based choices—not statistically optimized parameters. The result is a relative screening tool, not a prediction of future losses.

Annualized volatility

Value at Risk

A loss threshold at a selected confidence level under a chosen model.

Expected Shortfall

The average loss beyond a tail threshold, under the stated convention.

06 • MEAN VARIANCE FRAMEWORK

Markowitz optimization

The constrained optimizer minimizes annualized portfolio variance while respecting a minimum holding weight and an expected return floor.

WHY THIS OPTIMIZATION SETUP?

Make the objective and constraints interpretable

Why minimize variance?

The Markowitz framework links portfolio expected return to the weighted asset means and portfolio variance to the covariance matrix. Minimizing variance subject to a return floor asks for the least volatile allocation that meets a stated return requirement.

Why use the covariance matrix?

Individual stock volatility alone misses how stocks move together. Covariance captures those joint movements and is central to the diversification benefit in mean variance optimization.

Why keep a 1 percent minimum weight?

The floor prevents the optimizer from silently dropping a selected holding entirely while still allowing allocations to differ meaningfully from the equal weight baseline. It is a design constraint for this exercise, not a universal investment rule.

Why use equal weight as the baseline?

Ten percent per stock is simple, transparent and easy to reproduce. It gives a clear reference for judging whether optimization changes risk and allocation rather than confusing the result with an arbitrary starting portfolio.

Why use the TTM return as the target?

The TTM equal weight expected return provides a common reference target. Applying that target across regimes makes the comparison more consistent because each optimizer faces a comparable return requirement. Historical feasibility still depends on each regime's means and covariance, so the constraint must be checked for every run.

What is the main limitation?

Mean variance optimization is sensitive to estimated means and covariances. Small input changes can produce large weight changes, and the model does not directly account for trading costs, taxes or changing future relationships.

COVARIANCE MATRIX

Inspect the covariance inputs

Annualized covariance values are shown in decimal return units squared. Positive covariance means two assets tended to move together in the sample. Negative covariance means their observed movements tended to offset.

OPTIMIZATION RULES

How the allocation is chosen

Minimize wᵀ Σ w
  • Weights sum to 100 percent.
  • Each holding has a minimum weight of 1 percent.
  • Expected return must meet or exceed the target when feasible.
  • The objective is minimum variance, not maximum return.

WHY THIS COMPARISON?

Same holdings, different information sets

Holding the portfolio definition constant isolates how estimated risk and return change across observation windows. This is a retrospective comparison, not a live strategy simulation. Because the selected stocks and composite scores use a broad historical dataset, a strict historical performance claim would require point in time stock selection and parameter estimation using only information available before each test period.

INTERPRETATION

What changes from one window to another?

Because the portfolio selection uses a broad historical dataset, this comparison can contain lookahead bias. A true historical strategy test would need to select stocks and estimate parameters using only information available before each test period.
